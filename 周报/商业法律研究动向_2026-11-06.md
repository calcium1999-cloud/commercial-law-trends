### OBLB（2 篇）

#### Fintech in the Global South: Rewriting Regulatory Convergence in Financial Regulation
**作者**：Nydia Remolina Leon
**Affiliations**：
**摘要**：In February 2026, India’s Unified Payments Interface (UPI) processed more than 20 billion transactions in a single month, across nearly seven hundred participating banks. Nearly half of all real-time payments made worldwide in 2023 happened in India. In contrast, FedNow, the public instant payment system the United States launched in 2023, settled about 8.4 million payments in the whole of 2025, a volume UPI clears in roughly half an hour. The European Union secured universal instant euro transfers only by legislating in 2024, after years of uneven uptake. On the question of whether people can move money instantly, cheaply, and universally, the traditional financial centers now trail parts of the Global South. UPI, like Brazil’s Pix, is an interoperable rail that moves money in seconds between accounts at payment providers (banks and non-banks), at little or no cost to users. In much of the Global North, retail payments still run largely on card networks and slower bank transfers. Why did this architecture emerge in India and Brazil rather than in traditional financial centers? In a recent article , forthcoming in the American Journal of Comparative Law, I argue that UPI is no anomaly. Comparative financial regulation is usually narrated from the Global North, with New York, London, Frankfurt, and other financial centers, as the baseline toward which the rest of the world is presumed to converge. In several domains of fintech regulation, that premise is backwards.

#### Extending the Brussels Ia Regulation to Third State Defendants: a Gift from the EU to London’s Litigation Market?
**作者**：Johannes Ungerer
**Affiliations**：
**摘要**：Businesses, lawyers, and courts appreciate simpler rules, especially for cross-border litigation. But a simpler rulebook is not necessarily a better bargain. The European Commission’s current consultation on potentially reforming the Brussels Ia Regulation includes a particularly consequential question: should the European Union (EU) jurisdiction rules extend generally to defendants domiciled outside the EU? As I have argued , the answer should be no. Such a reform presented as harmonisation of jurisdictional rules could weaken EU businesses’ litigation options while handing an advantage to competing courts, particularly in London and other hubs overseas. The Commission’s 2025 Application Report and the new commercial courts in Member States make the question of possible extension more pressing. The issue is not whether uniformity looks attractive on paper, but whether it improves access to justice and commercial dispute resolution in practice. Brussels Ia determines which Member State’s courts may hear cross-border civil and commercial disputes and facilitates enforcement of their judgments across the EU. Its ordinary jurisdiction rules principally concern EU-domiciled defendants. For defendants domiciled elsewhere, such as in the UK post-Brexit, national rules generally decide whether a Member State’s court can hear the case.
