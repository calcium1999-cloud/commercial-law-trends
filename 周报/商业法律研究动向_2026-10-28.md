#### Value-Up Initiatives as a Strategy for Corporate Governance and Capital Market Development: Lessons from Asia
**作者**：Aurelio Gurrea-Martínez, Joon Hyug Chung, Nydia Remolina
**Affiliations**：
**摘要**：Over the past several years, a new form of corporate governance and capital market intervention has spread across Asia. Beginning with Malaysia and gaining international prominence following the Tokyo Stock Exchange’s 2023 initiative, several Asian jurisdictions, including Korea, Taiwan, Mainland China, Thailand and Singapore have adopted what this article broadly refers to as “value-up initiatives.” We provide a comparative assessment of these initiatives, emphasising their potential and limitations as a strategy for corporate governance and capital market development. The article starts by explaining why value-up initiatives have emerged so prominently in Asia and how they have been designed differently across the region. By encouraging boards to confront questions of capital allocation and valuation while improving the information available to investors, we argue that value-up initiatives can serve as a valuable corporate governance tool. Their effectiveness, however, depends on the institutional and market environment in which they operate, and they should be understood as complements, rather than substitutes, for effective legal and enforcement mechanisms for protecting investors. The article concludes by explaining how the Asian experience with value-up initiatives can offer valuable lessons for jurisdictions seeking to strengthen corporate governance while contributing to the development of capital markets.

#### Money Cop: What Thirty Years of Federal Reserve Enforcement Actions Reveal
**作者**：Dan Awrey, Jeffery Zhang
**Affiliations**：
**摘要**：It’s probably safe to say that the Federal Reserve is the most closely studied central bank in the world. Scholars have written at length about its monetary policy, its balance sheet, and its governance and independence. But far less attention has been paid to the Federal Reserve’s role as a federal law enforcement agency. Yet as one of four US federal banking agencies, the Federal Reserve supervises more than 3,000 banking organizations, collectively holding trillions in assets. How does the Federal Reserve actually use its enforcement powers? In our working paper, Money Cop , we try to answer that question in a systematic way by hand-collecting and cleaning all 350 public enforcement actions the Federal Reserve brought against a banking organization between 1995 and 2025. We coded each action by statutory authority, violation type, penalty and target, before matching the targets to regulatory filings and to daily stock returns. What we see raises important questions about how the Federal Reserve allocates its scarce enforcement resources, and how well its enforcement strategy supports its core price and financial stability mandates.

#### A Critique of Delaware’s AIC Draft Legislation
**作者**：Brian JM Quinn
**Affiliations**：
**摘要**：Against backdrop of high-profile news about AI agents going rogue and perhaps even being an existential threat to humanity, the State of Delaware is preparing to take a drastic step. Proposed draft legislation known as the Artificial Intelligence Company (AIC) Act would allow for the creation of a brand-new business entity managed entirely by an AI agent, operating without routine human supervision. Proponents claim that wrapping AI agents in a corporate shell will make AI ‘legible’ to the legal system. In reality, the AIC Act builds a near-impenetrable liability shield for Big AI (frontier AI model firms, like Anthropic, OpenAI, Meta, xAI, etc.). Rather than forcing Big AI to keep their autonomous agents under strict control, Delaware’s proposal creates a framework that actively rewards companies for engaging in less oversight of their AI agents. Proponents argue that as AI agents inevitably slip beyond human control, trying to ban them is futile, and that AI agents in the current form are an ill fit for the legal system and, as a result, beyond the law. Proponents of the AIC law propose that by wrapping AI agents in a corporate shell with legal personality one can overcome this infirmity. Under the AIC Act framework, an AIC can sue and be sued, hold property, enter into contracts, and incur debt in its own name.

#### From Contract Embellisher to Negotiation Machine: The Use of AI in Contracting
**作者**：Horst Eidenmüller
**Affiliations**：
**摘要**：In this post, I argue that AI tools can be used as a ‘Contract Embellisher’: the parties to a contract deploy them to improve an agreement they have already concluded. This is less risky and less complex than using AI to negotiate a contract in the first place. At the same time, experience with AI as a ‘Contract Embellisher’ can feed back into using AI as a ‘Negotiation Machine’. More than four decades ago, Howard Raiffa proposed the idea of ‘ Post-Settlement Settlements ’. Let’s assume A and B have concluded a contract. Is it value-maximizing, ie , efficient? Likely not, Raiffa hypothesized, especially in ‘complex negotiations where a lot of issues are at stake, where uncertainties are involved, or where settlements could involve transactions and payments over time’. In comes an ‘analytical intervenor’ from the ‘Contract Embellishment Service’. Think of this intervenor as a mediator or honest broker. The intervenor meets with A and B privately, probing them for confidential information about their interests and values. A and B may have been reluctant to disclose these fully in their bargaining. Then the intervenor solves a ‘mathematical optimization problem’, suggesting to the parties an improved contract which A and B are free to accept or reject. The intervenor’s services would be compensated with a small slice of the surplus created.
